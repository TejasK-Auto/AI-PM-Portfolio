# AI Product Manager Portfolio - Working Context

## Purpose and audience

This file is the durable working context for planning, writing, reviewing, and building an AI Product Manager portfolio in this workspace. The portfolio should help hiring teams and collaborators assess product judgment through evidence, decisions, execution, and learning—not through a pile of disconnected deliverables.

The intended positioning is a product manager who can move from a real user and business problem to a focused, validated, measurable product, and who can make sound choices about AI without treating AI as an end in itself. This is a working position, not a claim about a job title, shipped product, or personal achievement.

## Curriculum synthesis

### Module 1: Product foundations and discovery

Use this module to ground the portfolio in the PM's core job: maximize customer value while balancing business impact, technical feasibility, time, and resources. Show work across discovery, strategy, product definition, launch, measurement, and adaptation—not only feature delivery.

Relevant capabilities include:

- Understanding user needs, behavior, motivation, and context
- Choosing qualitative, quantitative, primary, or secondary research methods
- Conducting interviews without leading participants or pitching a solution
- Synthesizing evidence into personas, empathy maps, jobs-to-be-done, journey maps, pain points, and opportunity statements
- Comparing alternatives and assessing market and competitive opportunities
- Distinguishing a prototype (testing a design or interaction) from an MVP (testing whether a product solves a core problem in-market)
- Using desirability, feasibility, and viability to evaluate opportunities
- Testing risky assumptions with the smallest meaningful experiment

Portfolio implication: show the chain from user evidence to an opportunity and a decision. A research artifact without the decision it informed is incomplete.

### Module 2: Product definition, validation, and delivery

Use this module to show how a product idea becomes an aligned, testable plan and then executable work.

Relevant capabilities include:

- Translating a concept into a one-pager, PRD, user stories, and acceptance criteria
- Choosing the right fidelity: user flow, wireframe, mockup, prototype, or MVP
- Testing a concept or prototype with representative users and observing task completion, time, errors, assistance, confusion, and satisfaction
- Managing scope, dependencies, risks, and stakeholder expectations
- Using stakeholder maps, RACI, communication plans, and early escalation
- Turning a prioritized backlog into epics, stories, sprint plans, reviews, and retrospectives
- Making delivery realistic using historical evidence rather than optimistic estimates

Portfolio implication: connect each requirement and acceptance criterion to a validated user need or business outcome. Show how feedback, blockers, and cross-functional decisions changed the plan.

### Module 3: Strategy, market, business, and growth

Use this module to demonstrate that product decisions connect customer value to a viable business and a credible route to adoption.

Relevant capabilities include:

- Defining an ideal customer and estimating TAM/SAM/SOM with explicit assumptions
- Positioning against user alternatives and explaining how the product wins
- Separating vision, strategy, and roadmap
- Comparing cost-plus, value-based, subscription, freemium, usage-based, and tiered pricing approaches
- Planning the full GTM path: target, position, package, distribute, convert, and retain
- Diagnosing acquisition, activation, conversion, retention, revenue, and referral behavior
- Selecting growth loops, product-led growth, or sales-led growth based on the product and buyer
- Adapting priorities to launch, growth, maturity, or decline
- Defending a strategy with a claim, evidence, logic, trade-off, risk, and test

The Module 3 capstone material uses HireMate as a launch-strategy case. Treat it as a possible portfolio candidate only if it represents the owner's actual work; describe the assignment context, personal contribution, and which outputs are proposals rather than launched results.

### Module 4: AI product strategy, metrics, and responsible UX

Use this module to establish that AI choices are justified by a workflow and managed as product-quality and trust decisions.

Relevant capabilities include:

- Distinguishing an AI-added feature from an AI-native workflow redesign
- Selecting models and architectures based on the task, quality, latency, cost, context, privacy, licensing, and deployment constraints
- Using structured prompts and iterative evaluation rather than relying on one-off prompts
- Defining AI product success with a North Star, supporting metric levels, and guardrail metrics
- Evaluating outputs with appropriate reference-based and human evaluation, benchmarks, and red-team cases
- Designing for probabilistic output, latency, transparency, consent, user control, source attribution, graceful failure, and human review
- Considering fairness, reliability, safety, privacy, security, accountability, accuracy, grounding, and IP risks
- Avoiding unsupervised AI actions in consequential contexts

Portfolio implication: explain why AI is appropriate, what a non-AI baseline would do, how quality is measured, what happens when the system is uncertain or wrong, and who remains accountable.

## Portfolio narrative

Build a small set of complementary case studies that together prove the following:

1. **Discovery:** can identify and validate a meaningful problem.
2. **Product judgment:** can prioritize, define an MVP, and make explicit trade-offs.
3. **Execution:** can align stakeholders and translate a strategy into testable delivery work.
4. **Business thinking:** can connect positioning, pricing, GTM, growth, and lifecycle to customer value.
5. **Measurement:** can define outcomes, leading indicators, and guardrails before launch.
6. **Responsible AI:** can justify AI use, evaluate quality and risk, design trustworthy UX, and preserve human accountability.

Prefer two or three complete cases over a broad list of shallow projects. Include both AI and non-AI examples where the owner's real work supports them. Do not invent personal experience, project outcomes, user research, revenue, launch status, or metrics.

## Case-study working template

For each project, capture:

1. **Project facts:** name, type, dates, status, team, constraints, and the owner's role
2. **Problem:** target segment, user context, pain, frequency/severity, current alternatives, and why now
3. **Evidence:** source, method, sample, findings, limitations, and confidence
4. **Opportunity:** user value, business value, feasibility, market/competitive context, and priority
5. **Strategy:** target customer, positioning, desired outcome, non-goals, alternatives, and explicit trade-offs
6. **Solution:** concept, flow, wireframe/prototype, MVP boundary, and accessibility considerations
7. **Validation:** hypotheses, experiment or usability task, participants/data, measures, results, and resulting changes
8. **Definition and execution:** PRD, stories, acceptance criteria, dependencies, risks, stakeholders, and delivery plan
9. **Business and launch:** pricing/model rationale, packaging, channel, funnel, launch plan, and lifecycle assumptions
10. **Measurement:** metric tree, baseline, target, instrument/events, cadence, and counter/guardrail metrics
11. **AI-specific decisions, when relevant:** AI fit, model/architecture comparison, evaluation set, quality threshold, failure states, privacy/safety controls, transparency, and human review
12. **Outcome and reflection:** observed result or clearly labeled projection, lessons, remaining uncertainty, and next decision

Show selected artifacts inline or link to them with a sentence explaining what decision each artifact supports. Redact personal or confidential data.

## Evidence and integrity rules

Keep these categories distinct in every case:

- **Observed:** directly measured product behavior or recorded research evidence
- **Reported:** participant or stakeholder statement
- **Inferred:** interpretation supported by evidence
- **Assumed:** not yet validated
- **Proposed:** target, plan, or intended behavior
- **Simulated:** test or model output that is not real-world impact

For metrics, include a definition, unit, segment, time window, source, baseline, and target where applicable. Do not describe a proposed target as achieved, or a usability observation as statistically representative. State sample and method limitations.

For market sizing, pricing, or competitor claims, show the calculation or assumption and identify whether it has been independently verified. Do not present course examples as current market research.

## AI product quality checklist

When a case includes AI, cover the following:

### Product fit

- What user job or workflow is improved?
- Why is AI preferable to a rules-based, manual, or hybrid approach?
- What should remain deterministic?
- What are the quality, latency, context, cost, privacy, license, and deployment constraints?

### Evaluation and operations

- Define task success and output-quality criteria before launch.
- Use representative examples, failure cases, and human review; compare against a baseline where possible.
- Track grounding/correctness, hallucinations, harmful output, reliability, latency (including tail latency where relevant), cost per task, user satisfaction, trust, activation, retention, and task completion.
- Establish safety and quality guardrails, escalation paths, ownership, monitoring, and a rollback/degradation plan.
- Make clear which quality results are tested, simulated, or still unknown.

### User experience and responsible AI

- Disclose AI involvement in a useful, timely way.
- Give users control to review, edit, retry, undo, or decline AI suggestions.
- Explain sources or uncertainty when appropriate; request consent for data use and consequential actions.
- Design loading, empty, refusal, error, and recovery states; do not hide latency or failures.
- Use human approval for consequential decisions or actions.
- Assess fairness, privacy, security, misuse, safety, accuracy, accountability, and intellectual-property risks.

## Metrics and business logic

Use a metric hierarchy suited to the product rather than adding every framework:

- **North Star / outcome:** sustained user or customer value
- **Supporting metrics:** acquisition, activation, task success, conversion, retention, revenue, referral, or UX measures as appropriate
- **Leading indicators:** behaviors expected to move the outcome
- **Guardrails:** quality, safety, fairness, cost, latency, support load, or user harm measures

Use AARRR when diagnosing a growth funnel or HEART for a UX-heavy experience only when they help answer the case's question. For AI products, pair adoption metrics with quality and safety metrics; usage alone does not establish value.

## Working style for future portfolio work

- Begin by stating the audience, decision, and evidence needed.
- Ask for missing personal/project facts rather than inventing them.
- Label course-derived frameworks, personal analysis, assumptions, and external research.
- Use plain language and explain PM terminology in context.
- Prefer a few meaningful artifacts over decorative or redundant deliverables.
- Show what changed because of evidence and what would change the recommendation.
- Keep the portfolio site framework and hosting undecided until the content and intended audience are clear.
